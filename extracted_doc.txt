Method and evidence gradingJapanese craft-sector statistics are unusually messy for a sector this culturally prominent. METI itself concedes that industry-wide production and sales data is not maintained at a level adequate for analysis, which means the most-quoted numbers in the sector originate from a single industry association and are then recycled across media without re-verification. Executive summaryJapan has a sector with rising global demand, falling domestic supply, and almost no direct commercial connection between the two. That is the entire finding, and every number below is an elaboration of it.The supply side is in terminal declineProduction value in the METI-designated traditional crafts sector peaked at ¥541.0 billion (5,410億円) in 1983 and stood at roughly ¥105 billion (1,050億円) in FY2022 — about one-fifth of peak. The workforce fell from 287,956 in 1979 to 48,334 in FY2022; the number of businesses fell from 34,043 in 1979 to 16,704 in 2006. Of the remaining workforce, 63.6% is aged 50 or over and 18.0% is 70 or over, against 6.0% aged in their twenties or younger. This is not a soft demographic trend; it is a hard replacement failure inside one working generation.The demand side is at an all-time highIn calendar 2025 Japan received 42,683,600 inbound visitors who spent a record ¥9.4549 trillion — up 16.4% year on year, and roughly ¥1.3 trillion above 2024’s ¥8.1257 trillion. Separately, Japanese kitchen-knife exports rose from ¥2.5 billion in 2000 to a record ¥12 billion (~US$90 million) in 2021 — a 30% single-year jump, on 7.48 million knives, with makers publicly stating they cannot keep up with orders.The connection between them is missingMETI states plainly that e-commerce capability among traditional craft producers covers only about 20% of the sector («EC対応は全体の2割程度»), with channels still dependent on department stores and souvenir retail, and with an average firm size of 5.2 employees. This is not explained by the category being un-sellable online: the METI e-commerce survey puts the online penetration rate for the «household goods, furniture and interior» category at 32.58% — the third-highest of any physical-goods category in Japan. The shelf that crafts belong on is one of the most digitised in Japanese retail. The producers are not on it.The single most important number in this documentIn 2024, Chinese consumers alone purchased ¥2.6372 trillion of goods cross-border from Japanese businesses, up 8.5%. In the same year, Japanese consumers purchased only ¥441.0 billion cross-border from US and Chinese businesses combined. Japan is a net cross-border seller by roughly six to one, and the pull is coming from outside. Source: METI FY2024 E-Commerce Market Survey.2. How many of these businesses actually remain2.1 The designation systemThe statutory frame is the Act on the Promotion of Traditional Craft Industries (伝統的工芸品産業の振興に関する法律, Act No. 57 of 1974, commonly 伝産法). Under Article 2, the Minister of Economy, Trade and Industry designates individual craft items. Designation requires that the item be primarily for daily use, substantially handmade, produced by traditional techniques of 100 years’ standing or more, made from traditionally used raw materials, and geographically concentrated in a production area. The designated count stands at 244 items, up from 237 in March 2022 — the count rises even as the underlying industry contracts, because designation is a cultural-recognition act, not a commercial-health indicator. Reading the designation count as a proxy for sector vitality is the single most common analytical error in this space.2.2 The decline, quantifiedMetricPeakMost recentChangeProduction value¥541.0bn (1983)≈¥105bn (FY2022)≈ −81%Workforce287,956 (1979)48,334 (FY2022)−83%Businesses34,043 (1979)16,704 (2006)−51% (to 2006)Avg. firm size—5.2 employeesmicro-enterpriseWorkforce aged 50+—63.6%18.0% are 70+E-commerce capability—≈20% of firms—Two caveats on this table, both material. First, the business count of 16,704 is from 2006 — the most recent figure I could trace to a ministry document. Twenty years of subsequent attrition are not captured, so the true present-day figure is materially lower and nobody publishes it. Any business plan that treats 16,704 as a current addressable market is overstating it. Second, the FY2022 production value is contested: the association-derived figure is ¥105 billion, while METI’s own March 2026 policy deck presents ¥115 billion for a recent year, and a separate analysis puts FY2020 at ¥87 billion. The ~80% decline from peak is robust across all three; the current level is not precise to better than ±15%.2.3 Certified individualsAt the apex of the system, holders of Important Intangible Cultural Properties (重要無形文化財保持者, colloquially 人間国宝 / "Living National Treasures") are capped at 116 individuals by budget, each receiving a ¥2 million annual special grant to fund technique transmission. The cap is the point: national recognition is a fixed-size honour roll, not a scalable support mechanism. It preserves technique in individuals while the commercial base beneath them halves.3. Why they are not onlineThe common explanation — old craftspeople who dislike computers — is largely wrong, and believing it will produce the wrong product. The binding constraints are structural, and three of them are documented in METI’s own materials.3.1 The wholesale system severed makers from customersTraditional crafts historically moved maker → production-area wholesaler (産地問屋) → consumption-area wholesaler → retailer → customer. METI’s own analysis states that micro-scale producers «have no choice but to deliver at the price indicated by the production-area wholesaler» (零細事業者は産地問屋の示した価格で納めざるを得ない), and that only firms with strong direct-sales channels or independent maker-artists are able to raise their own prices. This is the deepest reason artisans never built customer-facing capability: for a century the system was designed so that they would not need it, and would not be permitted to profit from it. A maker who has never set a retail price, never photographed a product, never spoken to an end customer, and never held customer data is not one website away from e-commerce. They are an entire commercial function away from it.3.2 The firms are too small to absorb fixed costsAt an average of 5.2 employees, a craft business cannot amortise the fixed costs that cross-border e-commerce actually requires: product photography, English copy and product data, HS-code classification and customs paperwork, export-grade packaging design for fragile goods, payment and refund handling, and returns logistics. Each of these is a specialist function. None of them scales down gracefully to a five-person workshop making made-to-order goods.3.3 Japanese SME digitalisation: the real pictureThe widely repeated claim that around a quarter of Japanese SMEs have not begun digitalising at all is out of date. The 2025 SME White Paper reports that firms at Stage 1 (business conducted mainly on paper or verbally, no digitalisation) fell from 30.8% in 2023 to 12.5% in 2024, with Stage 2 (migrating to digital tools) rising from 35.4% to 52.3%. The White Paper itself cautions that the 2023 and 2024 surveys drew on different populations and are not strictly comparable.The important finding is the one underneath: Stage 4 — using digitalisation to transform the business model and build competitiveness — has not grown at all, and by the raw numbers has slightly declined. The two most-cited obstacles at every stage are «the cost burden is large» and «we lack the people to drive DX».What this means for product strategyBasic digitalisation is spreading through Japanese SMEs quickly and does not need solving. Transformation-level capability is stalled on cost and talent — precisely the two things a five-person workshop can never fix internally. A tool that asks a craft business to acquire new capability will fail. A service that supplies the capability — doing the photography, translation, listing, customs and logistics on the maker’s behalf — is aimed at the constraint the government’s own data identifies.3.4 Product-model mismatchMade-to-order and one-of-a-kind pieces do not map onto fixed-SKU inventory models. A kiln firing yields pieces that differ; the platform expects a stock count.Ceramics, lacquerware and glass are heavy, fragile and low-density in value-per-kilogram terms relative to shipping cost — the worst possible profile for international parcel economics.Raw-material dependency is a hidden fragility: several designated crafts depend on a handful of remaining domestic suppliers of specific lacquer, dye, silk or washi inputs, so material-supplier failure can extinguish a craft before its artisans retire.Pricing is genuinely hard. A maker who has only ever received a wholesale price has no basis for setting a retail price, and no data on what an overseas buyer will pay.4. Government efforts, and why they underperform4.1 The core subsidyThe Traditional Craft Industries Support Subsidy (伝統的工芸品産業支援補助金) is the sector’s primary instrument. Terms: subsidy rate of one-half or two-thirds, grant floor ¥500,000, grant ceiling ¥20 million. Eligible activity covers successor training, raw-material securing, cross-area and cross-sector collaboration, design development, exhibitions and market development in domestic and overseas consumption centres. For the FY2026 (令和8年度) budget, the subsidy sits as a component («内数») of a parent line of ¥1,082 million — approximately ¥1.08 billion, administered through METI’s regional bureaux. A separate disaster-recovery track offers up to ¥10 million at a three-quarters subsidy rate, with retroactivity to the date of the disaster so that producers who self-funded emergency repairs can still claim.4.2 The structural flaw in the subsidy designTo apply, an applicant must already have filed a qualifying statutory plan — a 振興計画, 共同振興計画, 活性化計画, 連携活性化計画 or 支援計画 — with METI, and it must be filed at least one month before the subsidy application itself. METI additionally publishes a guidebook on how to write these plans, which is itself evidence that applicants cannot write them unaided.This is the mechanism by which a well-funded programme misses its intended beneficiary. A five-person workshop with an owner in their seventies must, in Japanese, select among five plan archetypes, draft a statutory business plan, file it with a regional bureau, wait a month, and then submit a separate subsidy application. Cooperatives with administrative staff clear this. Individual micro-producers — the majority of the sector, and the ones actually disappearing — do not. The money is real; the gate is set at a height the target cannot reach.4.3 JETRO TAKUMI NEXT: the export-support programmeTAKUMI NEXT, running since FY2019, is the most directly relevant government programme to the e-commerce gap: it connects selected makers to overseas buyers who already sell Japanese crafts through physical retail and e-commerce, in the US, UK, South Africa, Australia, Hong Kong and elsewhere — roughly 20 partner buyers. For its seventh edition, TAKUMI NEXT 2025 adopted 106 companies from 34 prefectures, of which 74 were first-time participants. The prior edition, TAKUMI NEXT 2024, adopted 190 companies from 34 prefectures.Two observations follow, and both matter commercially. First, the programme contracted by 44% in one year (190 → 106 firms). Second, and more fundamentally: even at its 2024 peak of 190 firms per year, the programme reaches roughly 1% of the sector’s firms annually against a business base last officially counted at 16,704. Prefecture-level adoption data confirms the scale — Gifu, one of the strongest-performing prefectures, has received between 9 and 16 slots per year across five editions. TAKUMI NEXT is a well-designed curation programme operating at roughly one-hundredth of the scale of the problem.Assessment of government interventionThe instruments are competently designed and the money is not trivial. The failure is one of mechanism, not intent: subsidies are gated behind Japanese-language statutory planning that excludes micro-producers; grants are project-based rather than capability-building, so they fund an exhibition rather than a permanent sales function; and the one programme that directly addresses overseas channel access reaches roughly 1% of firms per year and just shrank by 44%. Public programmes are not competition for a private solution here. They are evidence of an unmet need, and a possible distribution partner.5. Reach versus demand: sizing the mismatch5.1 Inbound demandCalendar 2025 set records on both volume and value: 42,683,600 visitors (+15.8%) spending ¥9.4549 trillion (+16.4%), at ¥229,000 per general visitor. By origin, China led at ¥2.0026 trillion, then Taiwan (¥1.2110 trillion), the US (¥1.1241 trillion), Korea (¥986.4 billion) and Hong Kong (¥561.3 billion) — the top five accounting for over 62% of the total. Per-visitor spending is highest among long-haul Western markets: Germany at ¥393,710, the UK at ¥390,391 and Australia at ¥390,048. On shopping specifically, Singapore leads per-visitor at ¥100,816, followed by China (¥90,385) and Hong Kong (¥69,183).But crafts capture very little of it. In the survey’s shopping-category breakdown, «folk craft and traditional craft goods» (民芸品・伝統工芸品) ranks ninth, with a purchase rate of just 4.2% among Korean visitors and ¥13,603 spent per purchasing visitor. Thirty-six to forty-two million people per year walk through the world’s largest concentration of traditional crafts and 96% of them do not buy one. That is not a demand problem. It is a merchandising, discovery and price-legibility problem.5.2 The Japanese e-commerce contextSegment (Japan, CY2024)Market sizeYoYPenetrationBtoC-EC (total)¥26.1225 trillion+5.1%—— physical goods¥15.2194 trillion+3.70%9.78%— services¥8.2256 trillion+9.43%—— digital¥2.6776 trillion+1.02%—BtoB-EC¥514.4 trillion+10.6%43.1%CtoC-EC¥2.5269 trillion+1.82%—Household goods / furniture / interior¥2.5616 trillion+3.62%32.58%All figures from the METI FY2024 E-Commerce Market Survey, the 27th annual edition. Craft producers, at ~20% e-commerce capability, are absent from a channel their own category has already moved onto.5.3 Cross-border: where the demand actually sitsMETI’s survey measures cross-border e-commerce among Japan, the US and China. Japan is structurally a cross-border seller, and the buying pressure originates abroad.METI does not break out how much of the US consumer total (¥2.7144 trillion) comes from Japanese versus Chinese businesses, so the Japan→US figure cannot be stated. Any source that gives one is inferring it.5.4 The knife case: what happens when demand does connectJapanese kitchen knives are the sector’s clearest proof that the demand is real and the supply constraint is domestic. Exports rose from ¥2.5 billion (2000) to a record ¥12 billion (~US$90 million) in 2021, a +30% single-year increase on 7.48 million units and roughly +370% since 2000 — while the number of registered makers fell from over 2,000 (2007) to about 1,500 (2013), with makers stating on record that they cannot keep up with orders. This is the whole thesis in one category: demand five-folded, supply shrank, and the binding constraint was production and channel capacity — not interest.5.5 Proxy-buying services as revealed unmet demandBuyee, ZenMarket, FROM JAPAN and Neokyo exist for one reason: Japanese sellers frequently do not ship internationally or accept foreign payment cards. These services buy domestically on the customer’s behalf, consolidate, and re-ship, charging roughly 5–6% or ¥300–500 per item on top of both domestic and international shipping. An entire intermediary industry, charging a double-shipping premium, sustained purely by Japanese sellers’ inability to sell abroad, is the strongest available market signal in this research. Customers are paying a penalty to buy goods that producers are not selling to them.6. Who has tried, who is winning, and who failed6.1 The failure: generalist cross-border marketplacesRakuten Global Market, established in 2009, was the canonical attempt. It connected overseas shoppers to Rakuten Ichiba merchants and — importantly for this analysis — it already provided automatic translation, payment and delivery solutions. It closed permanently on 1 June 2020 at 14:00 JST after ten years, with Rakuten stating it was redirecting resources to strengthening local platforms and flagship-store partnerships in Taiwan, China and other markets. Rakuten’s US marketplace (formerly Buy.com) stopped taking orders on 15 September 2020; the UK marketplace had already been discontinued in August 2016.Contemporary trade coverage identified precisely what was lost: for Western buyers seeking small, obscure or niche Japanese brands without their own site or international shipping, the remaining options collapsed back to using a proxy service or physically visiting Japan.The lesson from Rakuten Global MarketTranslation, payment and international shipping were not sufficient. A generalist marketplace solves the transaction and leaves the hard parts untouched: curation, provenance, storytelling, and the per-item work of making a one-off handmade object legible and desirable to a foreign buyer. Any venture in this space that positions itself as "the marketplace for Japanese crafts" is proposing the model that already failed at Rakuten’s scale and with Rakuten’s balance sheet.6.2 The domestic modernisation success: Nakagawa Masashichi ShotenFounded in 1716 as a hemp-textile wholesaler in Nara, Nakagawa Masashichi Shoten converted itself into a vertically integrated manufacturer-retailer (製造小売 / SPA), moving from roughly ¥400 million in loss-making revenue in the early 2000s to ¥10.3 billion with 67 stores and around 618 employees, passing one million members. The company also consults for other makers and won the 2015 Porter Prize.The strategically significant detail is its definition of success. Nakagawa frames "revitalising crafts" as makers reaching profitability without relying on subsidies — an implicit verdict on §4 from the sector’s most commercially successful operator. Note also what the model required: taking over the retail function entirely. It did not digitise wholesalers; it replaced them.6.3 The cross-border success: Musubi KilnFounded in 2020 as JAPAN KUTANI SHOP and launching its English storefront in June 2021, Musubi Kiln sources directly from kilns in Kutani, Arita, Mashiko and Mino and handles English listings, international shipping, export-grade packaging and provenance verification — explicitly guaranteeing traceability against the counterfeits that global popularity has attracted. It is doing precisely what Rakuten Global Market did not: acting as an editorial and quality-assurance layer rather than a neutral pipe. Note that it is a curated retailer, not a platform — it takes inventory risk and merchandising responsibility, which is why it works.7. Where the structural gap actually isSynthesising the verified evidence, the gap is not a missing marketplace. Rakuten built that with translation, payment and logistics attached and shut it after ten years. The gap is a missing commercial function — the one the wholesale system used to perform and no longer does, which no five-person workshop can staff, and which government subsidies fund as one-off projects rather than permanent capability.7.1 The four unbundled jobsCuration and merchandising. Deciding which of 244 designated crafts and thousands of makers a specific overseas buyer should see, and in what context. Rakuten’s failure and Musubi Kiln’s traction both point here.Translation and storytelling. Not machine translation of a product name — the provenance narrative that converts an unfamiliar ¥40,000 tea bowl into a considered purchase. Rakuten had automatic translation and it was not enough.Provenance and authenticity. Musubi Kiln treats counterfeit risk as a named product feature. As global demand rises, verification becomes more valuable, not less — and it is not something an individual kiln can credibly self-assert.Fragile-goods logistics and customs. The unit economics that make ceramics and lacquer uneconomic to ship individually become tractable only under consolidation. This is the part the proxy-buying industry monetises today at a double-shipping premium.7.2 What the evidence rules outA generalist Japanese-crafts marketplace. Attempted at scale by Rakuten with better distribution and capital than a startup will have; closed 2020.A SaaS tool that asks makers to acquire digital capability. The SME White Paper identifies cost burden and absence of DX personnel as the binding constraints at every stage. A tool that requires the maker to supply the missing capability is aimed at the wrong side of the transaction.Domestic-only handmade commerce. Japan’s domestic CtoC-EC market grew just 1.82% in 2024 to ¥2.5269 trillion, while Chinese consumers alone bought ¥2.6372 trillion cross-border from Japanese businesses. The growth vector is outbound, not domestic.7.3 Sequenced approachStage 1 — Validate supply access, not demand (0–3 months)Demand is documented; it does not need validating. The unverified assumption is whether makers currently locked into wholesale distribution will supply a new channel. Target a category with proven export pull and tolerable shipping economics — knives are the strongest evidenced case (§5.4). Threshold to proceed: 10–20 makers with no existing English or international channel who will commit inventory. If supply access fails here, no amount of demand-side work rescues the model, and this is the cheapest possible place to discover that.Stage 2 — Build the missing layer as a curated retailer (3–12 months)Take inventory risk and merchandising responsibility, as Musubi Kiln and Nakagawa Masashichi both do, rather than operating as a neutral platform, as Rakuten did. Threshold to proceed: gross margin after international shipping and returns, plus repeat-purchase rate. If shipping destroys margin in the chosen category, change category before changing anything else — that is the constraint the proxy-buying industry’s fee structure is telling you about.Stage 3 — The B2B white space (12+ months)The clearest structural gap is wholesale: connecting Japanese makers to overseas retailers, restaurants and design studios. JETRO’s TAKUMI NEXT proves institutional demand exists — roughly 20 overseas buyers, actively sourcing — while simultaneously proving the gap, since it reaches around 1% of the sector’s firms per year and contracted 44% in 2025. There is no dominant private player. Threshold to proceed: 3–5 signed overseas buyer commitments before building anything.7.4 Conditions that would invalidate this analysisSignal to monitorImplicationSustained yen appreciationNarrows both the inbound and export arbitrage.
